Pima Indians Diabetes dataset · 768 patients

Predicting Diabetes from Eight Clinical Measurements

An end-to-end analysis of the Pima Indians Diabetes dataset, from the missing values through to what the final model ends up relying on.

By Fahim Ahamed  |  GitHub  |  Portfolio

Summary

Objective. Establish how well eight routine clinical measurements predict diabetes in this cohort, and whether the cohort contains unsupervised structure matching the diagnosis.

Data. The Pima Indians Diabetes Database: 768 women aged 21 and over, assembled for a 1988 paper1 from a National Institutes of Health study that measured this community's diabetes incidence at 19 times the rate of a mostly white population in Minnesota, the highest reported anywhere at the time3. 268 of the 768 women in the file have the disease. Eight measurements each, all obtainable in one appointment.

Approach. Disguised missing values are unmasked but filled only inside the model pipeline; the stratified 614/154 split is made before anything is fitted; four classifier families are grid-searched inside cross-validation and scored once on the held-out patients; four clustering algorithms are scored on chance-adjusted agreement with the diagnosis; the final model is examined with permutation importance, bootstrap coefficient intervals and per-patient attribution.

Findings. The four model families are statistically indistinguishable, separated by 0.0055 ROC AUC against 0.036 of run-to-run noise. Every rule for setting the decision threshold lands between 0.39 and 0.45, away from the 0.50 default. The cohort does contain a stable two-group structure, but it tracks age and metabolic load, agreeing with the diagnosis at only 0.1614. At the selected threshold the final model flags 45 of the 54 patients with diabetes among the 154 held out, and misses 9.

Section 01·Data cleaning

The file has no missing values, and that is the problem

The file opens clean. 768 rows, eight measurements each, no blank cells, zero duplicates. Then the minimums: blood glucose zero, blood pressure zero, body mass index zero. Nobody has those and is alive to be measured. Readings never taken, written into the file as 0.

There are 652 of them across five measurements. A model that does not notice treats a patient whose insulin was never drawn as one whose insulin is zero, and fits a population that does not exist.

One column has to be left alone. Pregnancies holds 111 zeros, every one a real answer. Blanking every zero would erase 14.5% of the most complete column.

How many gaps there are matters less than whether they are the same gap. Two columns missing a third of their values at random leave far more to work with than two that go missing together.

All of it is one event: a patient who did not get the full workup. That predicts two things, neither testable yet. Flags marking the missing values should add nothing, since they record the same fact. Estimating a gap from the other measurements should add nothing either: for these patients those are missing too.

Section 02·Exploratory analysis

One measurement does nearly all the separating

All eight measurements differ between patients with and without diabetes at levels most fields call highly significant, p-values from 1e-40 to 2e-06. That reads like eight useful signals. It mostly reflects sample size: with 768 patients a very small difference still clears the bar.

Cohen's d asks the screening question instead: how large is the difference next to how much the measurement varies between people anyway.

An effect size compares two averages, so it cannot say whether risk climbs steadily across a measurement's range or only bites past some point. For a screening rule that decides whether a simple cut-off would do.

A raised blood glucose reading is close to the definition of type 2 diabetes, so a model leaning on glucose is partly rediscovering a diagnostic criterion. Keep that in mind wherever glucose appears.

Section 03·Evaluation design

Fixing the rules before fitting anything

The decisions that move a result like this most are made before a model is fitted, and are the easiest to make by accident. Three rules hold throughout.

  • The split comes first. 614 patients to learn from, 154 set aside. Anything that learns from data, gap-filling averages included, is refit inside each cross-validation fold.
  • Nothing is chosen on the held-out patients. Model settings and the cut-off both come from the training half. The rest are scored once, at the end.
  • Accuracy is read against a baseline. 65.1% of this cohort does not have diabetes, so answering "no" to everyone scores 65.1% and finds nobody.

One tempting shortcut breaks this dataset: filling a missing glucose with the average glucose of patients with the same diagnosis. The model then predicts the answer from a number derived from it, scores well, and fails on the first real patient.

Both hold. The spread is 0.0046 ROC AUC against 0.035 of run-to-run noise, so the choice disappears into the uncertainty. Iterative filling costs far more to run and buys nothing, for the reason section 01 gave: it estimates each missing value from measurements that are missing too.

The rest of the analysis uses the median with flags, the simplest defensible default. Decisions like this absorb a lot of effort in practice. Measure once and stop.

Section 04·Model comparison

Four model families, one score

Four model families, picked to differ in what they can represent. Logistic regression draws one straight boundary. A support vector machine bends it. A random forest and a gradient-boosted ensemble cut the space into arbitrary regions and catch interactions. If a simple model is missing structure here, the flexible ones have the machinery to find it.

They land between 0.8297 and 0.8352, a range of 0.0055. Re-running any one on a different split of the same patients moves its score by about 0.036, roughly 7 times the distance between first place and last.

Reading a winner off these numbers is reading noise.

A second measurement agrees. Plain logistic regression placed 3rd in cross-validation and first on the held-out patients at 0.8569. The models did not change between those results. The sample did.

The ceiling belongs to the data. Section 02 showed the two groups overlapping across almost the whole range of every measurement, and no algorithm separates patients who look alike on its inputs. A tuned model reaches 77.0% accuracy against 65.1% for answering "no" to everyone: about 12 points, bought by finding most of the cases.

The cut-off is the decision that matters to a clinic, and every clinic answers it differently.

Section 05·Threshold selection

Choosing the decision threshold

A model returns a number between 0 and 1. Turning that into "call this patient back" needs a cut-off, and every library defaults to 0.50. That default claims a missed case costs the same as a false alarm. A false alarm costs one confirmatory test. A missed case costs the years before the next one.

Four ways of choosing, all computed on the training half so the held-out patients stay sealed.

F10.4318133105

Moving from the cost-weighted cut-off, which prices one missed patient at five false alarms, to the strictest rule turns 16 caught cases into missed ones and saves 24 false alarms. Whether that is a good trade depends on what a confirmatory test costs the clinic and what a late diagnosis costs the patient. Shipping 0.50 answers it without asking.

At the cut-off used here, 0.43, the final model flags 45 of the 54 patients with diabetes among the held-out 154. It raises 31 false alarms and misses 9. Moving the line does not fix those 9.

Section 06·Clustering

What the unsupervised groups turn out to be

That was the supervised half. The other half hides the diagnosis and asks whether the cohort sorts itself into groups at all, and whether those groups are diabetes. If they were, a clinic could triage without labeled training data.

Four methods that assume different things: k-means looks for round groups of similar size, a Gaussian mixture allows stretched overlapping ones, Ward linkage builds a hierarchy with no notion of a center, and DBSCAN hunts for dense regions and may leave most of the cohort unassigned. Real structure should show up in most of them. The diagnosis only scores the answer afterward.

DBSCAN gives the plainest evidence: 1 group, 27 patients unassigned, no dense regions with sparse ground between them. Ward linkage scores -0.0165, fractionally worse than chance.

The groups are not empty, though. Splitting the cohort in two is highly repeatable: resample the patients, refit, and the same boundary returns with an agreement of 0.83.

A clinic could use this to decide who to watch, and could not use it to decide who has diabetes.

Section 07·Validation check

Why the better-scoring clustering is the wrong one

There is a version of section 06 that reports the opposite, and it is the version a careful-looking analysis produces by default.

Grouping patients requires a notion of distance between them. On the measurements as they arrive, that distance is dominated by whichever one spans the widest numeric range. Here that is insulin, running to 846, while the diabetes pedigree score never passes 2.42. The distance between two patients is very nearly the distance between their insulin readings.

Done that way, the result scores far better on the standard internal check.

A silhouette score asks whether a boundary is clean. It cannot ask whether the boundary separates anything, and on raw measurements it rewards the wrong answer confidently. An analyst who checked the silhouette and stopped would have shipped the result backwards, holding a number that looked like validation.

The rule that caught it is the one to carry out of this project. No single measure is trusted until a second, independent one agrees. Section 04 survives it: the cross-validated ranking and the held-out ranking both say the differences sit inside the noise. Section 06 survives it: the groups score high on repeatability and low on agreement with the diagnosis. The raw-measurement clustering fails it, and appears here as an artifact.

Section 08·Explainability

What the final model relies on

When performance cannot choose between models, what they can tell you decides. Take the linear model and the forest, which scored the same by different routes.

Age and pregnancies are correlated here, and the two models divide that shared information differently. The forest can bend age into a curve and takes it directly. The linear model cannot, so some of the same signal reaches it through pregnancies. Equal accuracy, different explanations, and a report showing only one would read as though it were the explanation.

A single fit cannot say how much a number would move on different patients. Refitting the pipeline on bootstrap resamples answers that.

The measurements share a scale before fitting, so these numbers compare directly. A one standard deviation rise in blood glucose, about 31 mg/dL, multiplies the odds of diabetes by 2.85, plausible range 2.44 to 3.47.

One patient at a time

Averages do not tell a clinician why a particular person was flagged, the question a patient in the room asks. For a linear model the answer is exact: each measurement's push adds up to the prediction.

The harder case is one of the 9 misses. Patient 284 has diabetes and scored 0.23, below the 0.43 cut-off. Every measurement the model relies on sits below average: glucose 108 against a cohort average of 122, BMI 27 against 32, 2 pregnancies against 3.8. One value stands out: she is 52 against an average of 33, and age is the one measurement whose range crosses zero for this model.

She is section 02's overlap made concrete. On the inputs available she looks like a patient without the disease, and no cut-off catches her: reaching 0.23 would flag a large part of the cohort with her. What would catch her is a measurement this file does not contain. A screening question ends there, at a different visit instead of a better algorithm.

Conclusions

What the project found

  1. Four different model families scored within 0.0055 of one another while the noise on any one was 0.036. There is no winner to pick.
  2. The held-out patients agreed. Their uncertainty ranges run about 0.13 wide, and every model's score falls inside every other model's range.
  3. Gap-filling made no measurable difference, and section 01 explained why before section 03 measured it: the gaps are all one event.
  4. Every rule for setting the cut-off landed between 0.39 and 0.45, outside the library default of 0.50.
  5. The cohort does not divide into diabetic and non-diabetic groups. It divides along a repeatable gradient of age and metabolic load that diabetes follows only loosely.
  6. The standard quality check for that clustering preferred the version that recovered nothing. A second, independent measure is what caught it.
  7. Only 4 of 13 inputs have an effect distinguishable from zero, and two equally accurate models disagree about which measurements matter.
  8. The 9 patients the model misses look ordinary on these eight measurements, which no cut-off or algorithm repairs.

Limits

What this data cannot tell us

  • Who it describes. 768 women of Pima heritage, aged 21 and over, from a community with an exceptionally high diabetes burden3. Both the base rate and the relationships between measurements may differ elsewhere.
  • Whose records these are. A community under sustained research attention for decades. The file is used here as the public teaching dataset it has been since 1988, for a methods exercise.
  • Its age. Assembled for a 1988 paper1. Diagnostic criteria, measurement practice and treatment have all moved since.
  • How precise any of it is. 154 held-out patients give uncertainty ranges about 0.13 wide. Several conclusions above are limited by that and not by the modeling.
  • Insulin. 48.7% of it is estimated, so anything the model says about insulin is partly a statement about the estimate.
  • Glucose is close to the diagnosis. Its dominance throughout is substantially a definition being rediscovered.
  • Cause. Every relationship here is an association measured at one point in time. They describe who, without explaining why.
  • The scores rank patients; they are not risks. Balanced class weights improve how many cases the models catch and distort the probabilities. A score of 0.70 does not mean a 70% chance.
  • It is not a diagnostic tool. Nothing here is medical advice.

Assumptions and checks

  1. Zeros in five measurements record an absent reading. Checked by confirming each is biologically impossible, and by leaving Pregnancies out, where zero is a real answer for 111 patients. A unit test fails if the rule widens.
  2. The gap-filling method does not affect the result. Checked across six combinations: the spread was 0.0046 against 0.035 of noise.
  3. Outliers are real patients. 84 rows (10.9%) sit outside the usual fences, concentrated in the two most skewed measurements. They are kept, and handled by scaling and model choice.
  4. Two groups is a reasonable number to look at. Four criteria disagree: the silhouette prefers 2, Davies-Bouldin 10, the mixture model's BIC 5. The disagreement is itself the finding, and section 06 does not rest on the number.
  5. The final model is interchangeable with the others. Checked on two independent measurements, cross-validated and held-out, which agree that the differences sit inside the noise.

Reproducibility

An installable Python package (pandas, scikit-learn, Plotly) with per-module unit tests generates everything above. Every number is interpolated from the processed data at build time and none is typed by hand. Scores are ROC AUC unless stated otherwise; cross-validated figures are means over repeated 5x5 stratified folds on the training half, and held-out figures are scored once.

  1. Source. One file, 768 rows, from the UCI Machine Learning Repository2 and committed unchanged. No merges or joins.
  2. Cleaning. Zeros in Glucose, BloodPressure, SkinThickness, Insulin and BMI become missing, 652 values across five columns; Pregnancies is untouched. No rows dropped, 0 duplicates found. The processed file keeps its gaps: filling them learns from data, so it belongs inside the pipeline where it is refit on every fold.
  3. Analysis. Descriptive statistics and effect sizes; a stratified 614/154 split; grid search inside 5-fold cross-validation, then repeated 5x5 stratified cross-validation for the reported scores; percentile bootstrap intervals on the held-out set; four clustering algorithms scored on chance-adjusted agreement; permutation importance and bootstrap coefficient intervals.
  4. Checks. A test asserts the scaler is fitted on training rows only and fails if gap-filling moves outside the pipeline. Another recomputes the numbers quoted on this page and fails the build on a mismatch. Seeds are fixed throughout, and results are identical on scikit-learn 1.5 and 1.8.
